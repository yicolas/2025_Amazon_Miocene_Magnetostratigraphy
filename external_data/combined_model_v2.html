8. Foreland flexural basin model (thrust loading)

Classical elastic plate deflection under Andean thrust-belt topographic load (Beaumont 1981; Turcotte & Schubert 2002). This model captures foredeep subsidence near the thrust front — it does NOT model the Fitzcarrald Arch, which is driven by basal shear (see §9).

⚠ Bishop et al. (2018) show the arch is a basal-shear feature, not a flexural forebulge. This section tests and demonstrates that the flexural model alone fails to explain arch uplift rates.

Flexural equations

w(x) = w0 · e−x/α · cos(x/α)
α = [ 4D(ρm−ρf)g ]1/4    D = E·Te³12(1−ν²)
U(x) = wMio(x) − wnow(x)Δt

Broken plate solution: deflection decays exponentially with distance from load, oscillating through a forebulge. Compares two time-steps to estimate uplift from load reduction.

GPa — stiffness of lithosphere. Standard: 70–100 GPa. Combined with Te determines flexural rigidity D.
Typically 0.25 for crustal rock. Weak sensitivity.
kg/m³ — asthenospheric mantle restoring the plate. Bishop (2018) uses 3200–3300.
kg/m³ — average density of sediment + water filling the foredeep. Bishop uses ~2700 for crust, but basin fill is lighter.

9. Basal shear & Airy isostatic model (Bishop et al. 2018)

The Fitzcarrald Arch is driven by ductile crustal thickening from below — the flat Nazca slab drags the base of South American crust westward via basal shear, thickening the lower crust by ~5 km beneath the arch (Bishop et al. 2018). This model computes the resulting isostatic surface uplift and implied uplift rate.

This is the mechanism that Bishop et al. (2018) show actually explains the arch — not flexural forebulge dynamics (§8).

Airy isostasy equation

hsurface = Δhcrust × ρm − ρcρm

For every km of crustal thickening from below, the surface rises by (ρm−ρc)/ρm of that amount. With ρm=3300, ρc=2700: 1 km thickening → ~182 m surface uplift.

Basal shear profile (Gaussian dome)

Δhcrust(x) = Δhmax · exp(−(x−xarch)² / 2σ²)

Crustal thickening modelled as a Gaussian dome centered on the arch crest. Width σ controls how broadly the thickening is distributed. Based on Bird (1984) kinematic model scaled by Bishop et al. (2018) to the Nazca Ridge geometry.

Marker city structural positions along the profile (SW → NE):
▎Pto Tahuantinsuyo (130 km, 269 m) — deep foredeep, closest to the sub-Andean thrust front. Highest elevation of any city on the transect due to proximity to the Andean topographic front. Dominated by flexural subsidence; sedimentary fill is actively accumulating.
▎P. Maldonado (180 km, 183 m) — foredeep to proximal forebulge transition. Lower elevation than Pto Tahuantinsuyo despite being further from the Andes — the river has descended off the sub-Andean ramp. Flexural subsidence still significant; basal shear contribution is minor.
▎Sepahua (250 km, ~290 m) — NW flank of the Fitzcarrald Arch, where the Urubamba River crosses onto the arch. Elevation is higher than adjacent basins (~120 m asl), confirming arch uplift extends to this position. Transitional between foredeep and arch crest.
▎Fitzcarrald Arch crest (350 km, ~400–500 m) — the arch apex, located above the shallowest segment of the flat Nazca slab. Maximum crustal thickening (~5 km) and highest surface uplift. No significant faulting — deformation is entirely ductile in the mid-to-lower crust (Bishop et al. 2018).
▎Pto. Esperanza (400 km, ~233 m) — NE (distal) flank of the arch, on the Alto Purús drainage toward the Amazonian lowlands. Past the arch crest, elevation decreases as crustal thickening wanes and the slab re-steepens into the mantle.
▎Riberalta (500 km, 130 m) — distal foreland / backbulge, at the Madre de Dios–Beni confluence in Bolivia. Well past the arch, elevation has returned to the regional lowland level (~120–150 m asl). Minimal flexural or basal shear influence at this distance.
